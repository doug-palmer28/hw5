Reflection (Problem 10)

Doug's reflection on the live session, expanded with evidence from the ticket tabs, the Cash tab, and the audit trail (seq 432–565). Labels: Can today the current app can already do it · Partly some of it works today · Needs new tools it can't be done without new data or tools.

How the agents performed

I thought the agents performed well. They solved the tasks, they never went into the red, and they asked for my approval whenever they needed it.

  • They solved the tasks. Each ticket ended in the right state: 102 resolved, 101 waiting on vendor (the tee can't arrive because the shop date never moves past Aug 31), and 103 in progress (the club still has to pick a quantity). None of them was closed early.
  • They never went into the red. Checking went $3,400 → $2,560 → $160, and nothing was ever refused for lack of cash. In the very first loop on ticket 101, Accounting pointed out that paying the invoice and the rent would leave exactly $160, so the team knew the whole cash picture before I approved anything.
  • They asked before acting. Three requests came to me (#1 $840, #2 $2,400, #3 the $49.30 hoodie price), and nothing happened without my click. Both payments say approved_by = Doug. The agents could only request; the amounts came from the database, and the pay tools aren't in any agent's toolbox.
Evidence: the Final status and Human decision rows on the 101, 102, and 103 tabs; the Cash tab ledger and its database check.

Where the run differed from the plan

For the invoice and the rent, the agents did almost exactly what I predicted: the same agents, the same tools, the same amounts, and the same end states. The one difference on both was that the boss sent two workers at the same time (Inventory + Accounting on 101, Facilities + Accounting on 102) instead of one after the other. On 101 that meant Accounting looked up the invoice itself instead of reading Inventory's note first. On 102 Accounting read the lease itself before asking for payment. It cost a couple of extra lookups, but nothing went wrong, because no money could move without me.

The discount for the AI Club changed more, and that came from a feature of the app: when I start the team I can leave the boss a message. On 103 I wrote "See if we have enough and if we can afford to this one. We are running low on cash." The audit trail shows the boss still sent Inventory first, and then brought in Accounting with Inventory's count. My message didn't change the order; it changed the question. Both workers were told to look at affordability, and Accounting came back with a cash answer I hadn't planned for: restocking the 12 missing hoodies at the $22 cost would be about $264, which is $104 more than the $160 we had left. The other change from my plan was that the discount was filed for 20 hoodies, not the 8 actually on the shelf. Accounting marked it "subject to supply," and the reservation tool can never set aside more than 8, but I would rather it had asked about 8.

Evidence: "Matched the plan?" on each ticket tab; "Doug's instructions" and "Discount proposed" on the 103 tab.

Would one agent with all the tools be simpler?

I think some of this would have been simpler with one agent and all the tools, so there wasn't as much back and forth between the agents. Inventory and finance especially are so closely related that they had to talk to each other a lot, and that could have been avoided if everyone could see the inventory information.

The numbers back this up. About half of every ticket's tool calls were coordination (reading the ticket, reading or writing the team's notes, and handing off work) rather than real work:

The ticket was read 21 times across the three tickets. And Accounting has no stock tool at all: on 103 it priced a hoodie order using only the count the boss relayed from Inventory, and that is how a discount for 20 got filed while only 8 were on the shelf.

The separate roles did earn their keep in one place, though: safety. Only Accounting could ask for money, only Facilities checked the rent notice against the lease, and Customer Service could only write drafts. With one agent holding every tool, those boundaries would rest on a single prompt. The middle ground I'd try first is to keep the roles but share the read-only facts: give Accounting and the Boss check_stock, and give Customer Service get_cash_position. The workers would talk less, and the pay and approve tools would stay walled off.

More problems this team could take on

Can today 1. Customer service answers questions about wait times (paying our suppliers, and when customers get their shirts)

Mostly possible right now. Customer service already has get_invoice, which reports whether a supplier bill is paid, the vendor's lead time, and the expected arrival date, plus list_approval_requests to see whether a payment is still waiting on me. On 101 it did exactly this: draft #1 said no date could be promised while the invoice was unpaid, and draft #2 said "earliest arrival Sept 5, an estimate, not a guarantee."

  • What's missing: a way for a customer to ask. Questions only arrive as tickets, and the only types are order, rent, and price override. A "customer question" ticket type would make this routine.
  • Limit: it can say whether a supplier has been paid, but not when we will pay. That's my decision, and nothing records a planned payment date.

Partly 2. Propose trade-offs across tickets ("pay the rent or pay the supplier?") and bring them to me to decide

The reasoning is already there: get_cash_position shows every bill competing for the same cash, and Accounting's prompt tells it to rank bills when they compete. On 101 it did: "paying both leaves $160, so rent should remain the priority." In this run both bills fit, so there was no real conflict to choose between ($840 + $2,400 = $3,240 ≤ $3,400).

  • What's missing is the structure, not the data. The app works one ticket at a time (runs queue behind one lock), and each approval card is a single yes/no. To get a real "Option A vs. Option B" choice, it would need a planning run across all open tickets and a decision card that offers options, like "pay rent now, pay the reprint after," each with the cash left over.
  • This idea will matter more once money can come in (see the cash-flow idea below). Then when to pay, not just whether, becomes the real question.

Needs new tools 3. A short status brief for the leadership team (cash, inventory, tickets), drafted by customer service

This sounds easy, but customer service can't do it today:

  • It has no cash tool (get_cash_position is only for the Boss, Accounting, and Facilities) and no ticket list (list_tickets is Boss-only).
  • Inventory can only be checked one product at a time (check_stock(sku)). Nothing lists every product, so the mug being sold out wouldn't come up unless someone asked about mugs.
  • draft_customer_message has to be attached to a ticket, and customer service's prompt specifically forbids sharing cash or costs, which is right for customers but wrong for an internal brief.

What would make it work: a list_inventory tool, read-only cash and ticket access for whoever writes it, and an "internal report" draft that isn't tied to a ticket and has its own audience rules. The Boss is the more natural author, since it already sees cash and every ticket. Here's what a brief from this run would say: checking $160 with nothing owed; stock at cost $1,024 (40 caps, 32 hoodies, 10 tees, 0 mugs); 101 waiting on the vendor, 102 resolved, 103 waiting on the club.

What the team would need new tools for

Needs new tools 1. Plan purchases from demand history, and place orders with vendors

I agree: this isn't possible today. The database has three tickets and no sales or demand history, and no tool can create a vendor order. Every restock in this run was either an invoice that already existed (101's reprint) or just an estimate. On 103, Inventory said "no restock is confirmed because the ticket has no linked invoice," and Accounting could only estimate that restocking 12 hoodies would cost about $264.

  • New pieces: an order/demand history table, a forecast tool, and a request_purchase_order tool.
  • One guardrail matters most: a purchase order commits cash just like a payment, so it should go through the same human approval and the same "never go below $0" check. On 103 it would have been blocked anyway: $264 > $160.

Partly 2. A cash-flow plan: when cash comes in, when fixed costs hit, and when we'll be lean

As I said, it can already do this for rent. get_cash_position lists the rent still due (with its date), every unpaid invoice, and every request in flight, and the team used that to see the $160 floor before I approved anything. But two things stop a real plan:

  • Money never comes in. That's one of the simulation's rules, so there is literally nothing to forecast on the income side. On 103, a $986 sale would not have raised the balance at all. Customer payments would need a sales/receivables table and a tool to record income.
  • Time never moves. The shop date stays at 2026-08-31, so there's no "next month." A plan needs future dates, recurring costs beyond the one lease (payroll, utilities), and a way to step the calendar forward.

This one can start today, more than I expected. The pricing table already has unit cost and list price for every product, and check_stock gives stock on hand, so the team can work out margin per unit and how much money is sitting on the shelves:

Bottom line

The team was trustworthy: every dollar went through me, the cash math reconciles to the penny, and the agents were honest about what they couldn't promise (no tee date before payment, no hoodie quantity they didn't have). The biggest costs were coordination overhead and an Accounting agent that couldn't see stock. The first fixes I'd make: share read-only stock and cash data across roles, add a cross-ticket planning run with option-style decisions, and then add history and ordering tools, keeping purchase orders behind the same approval gate as payments.
